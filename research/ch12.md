## 2. The concept as the field holds it

**What a "declared language" means in the field today.** Two families. (1) *Language workbenches and parser generators*:
JetBrains MPS, Xtext, Spoofax, and Racket's `#lang` give you the means to define a language with its grammar, editor and
checks. The estate's field study (`D:\GitHub\ringua\docs\landscape.md`, "founding analysis, 2026-08-06") says why they did not win:
"died of ecosystem gravity" (§1.2), and Racket's languages "live and die inside Racket" (§1.6). (2) *Internal DSLs*: a library
whose calls read like sentences while the file can still hold any host code; the same study: "closedness is the product. An
internal DSL that cannot refuse arbitrary host code is an API wearing a language's costume" (§1.3). Its control group, the
closed languages that won: SQL, regex, Make, HTML and CSS, HCL, GraphQL, Markdown, CUE (§2). Chapter 11 already tells this;
chapter 12 should take it as read and say only what is new: *what produces a closed language*.

**What the field has for "the grammar a model must obey".** The record names one tool: llama.cpp's grammar sampler, "the
reference implementation of GBNF"; the library's machine "follow[s]" its element encoding and algorithm and "Nothing is
vendored" (`base\neural\SOFTANZA_STRUCTURED_OUTPUT.md` §7.3, 2026-08-22). General knowledge, to be marked as such in the chapter:
model vendors offer JSON-schema "structured output" modes at their API, and open libraries compile a JSON schema or a regular
expression into a token mask for a local model. The record holds no dated study of any of them. The record's own comparison is
the contract C9 itself: the same promise made locally, plus the court half and the list of what the grammar cannot carry.

**What the field has for "a factory of languages".** Nothing the record cites as a product that, from one declaration, emits
parser, court, grammar and runtime *and* governs agents. The nearest practices are the workbenches above (which emit parser
and editor, not a court or a model's mouth) and the practice of compiling a spec to a conformance suite. The record's study
says the novelty is the pairing: "Every prior wave paid two costs it could not lower: building the compiler ... and bounding
the users" and the model era "inverts both at once" (`landscape.md` §4). I found no source for any further claim about the
field; the chapter should make none.

```haro

	[ :field = "note",     :type = :string, :optional = 1 ]

#      note : string [optional]

#    dish: <one of: margherita | tiramisu>

#--> 0
? aV[:findings][1][:message]
```

## 10. The exercise

Judged by the tutor's rule: never answered first. Not the loan request of chapter 11. This one makes the reader the grammar.

```haro exercise
# Write, by hand, a grammar for one line of a lunch order:  "dish: " then one of two dishes of your choice, then a newline.
cG = 'root ::= ...'     # your grammar here
# Before you run anything, write your four predictions as promises:
? StzGrammarAccepts(cG, "dish: <your first dish>" + nl)
#--> ?
? StzGrammarAccepts(cG, "dish: <a dish not on your menu>" + nl)
#--> ?
? StzGrammarCouldContinue(cG, "dish: <the first letters of your first dish>")
#--> ?
? StzGrammarAccepts(cG, "dish: <the first letters of your first dish>")
#--> ?
? StzGrammarRefusal(cG) = ""
#--> ?
```

Then add a number to the line, `quantity: ` and digits, and write a fifth prediction: does your grammar accept `quantity: 4000`? Run it.
The lesson is the third and the fifth: the machine already knows a prefix could continue and is not yet a sentence, and it cannot know the
number is too large. Write down, in one sentence, who in your restaurant must refuse forty portions, since the grammar cannot. (I ran the
equivalent in K2 to K4; the reader's grammar will differ, the four answers will not.)
