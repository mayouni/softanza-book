## 3. The first principle

The sentence the record returns to, in the doctrine document (`Softaza and Agents That Cannot Hurt You.md`, "The problem nobody is solving from the right end"; undated, no author line):

> "We will not build safe agents. We will build a safe world, and let ordinary agents loose inside it."

The four-step form (study 12.29, line 1217) is the chapter's subtitle: "The agent proposes, the workbench rehearses, the court judges, only a governed actor commits." (`D:\GitHub\stzsite\content\en\agentic.md`, "The Softanza way".)

The framework document (`Softanza Virtual System Framework.md` §2) holds the two principles the chapter rests on: "**P1 — Safety by Construction.** The virtual layer holds no reference to reality." and "**P6 — Separated Powers.** ... By default, no actor holds both" (imagining and enacting).

Suggested move: chapter 17 said the model proposes. This chapter says what *proposes* means in the code: a plan, an object with operations, a narration, a list of flags, a verdict on who may commit it.

## 5. The decision, and what was refused

The library's design documents are written in the author's project, by the author or by sessions working under him; the record does not mark which. I mark "session" only where the document says so.

## 9. Six doors

- **The programmer:** your agent is a file and a few functions; it runs, believes it acted, and the disk does not move; you read the plan, you choose the committing actor and the scope, and the court tells you in one sentence which rule refused you.
- **The analyst:** every proposal is a narrated plan of operations, with the destructive steps flagged and an audit line for each commit: you can read what the agent meant to do, step by step, without running it. (The flags are by kind, not scored.)
- **The designer:** the plan is the one artefact that crosses between the agent and the person, and a person can reject one step. The Right to a Human (rule 102) says a person must be reachable, clearly and without penalty, wherever a machine serves; the interface law holds it by judgment, not by a machine.
- **The decision-maker:** you declare what each agent covers and how reversible it is before it may exist in the loop, and you decide the scope and the actor that commits. A model is structurally unable to commit, even when fooled (threat model T3, "assumed to happen"). The hardest part of your decision is the one the design leaves to you: which functions code allows an agent to call (G35).
- **The educator:** a learner's agent that "tidies" a course folder by deleting everything is a lesson in what a plan is: she sees what it would have done and the files are still there. (The source is the education demo's scene; the book's block 6 is the small form.)
- **The agent:** you may rehearse without limit, inside the twin, and your only export is a plan you can be argued with over. You may not commit. Today the argument is one-way: you do not yet receive the reviewer's objection and revise (`Receive`/`Revise` are designed).
