## 2. The concept as the field holds it

Two things are called "style" in the field, and the chapter has to say which one it means.

**Style as layout, enforced by a tool.** Python's style guide (PEP 8, 2001), Go's formatter (`gofmt`, 2009) and the linters every
project runs before a commit (general knowledge; the same sources chapter 5 names). They judge the shape of a line: spaces, capitals,
line length, unused names. They are very good at it and they run by default. They say nothing about whether a line follows the order
in which its writer reasoned.

**Style as a small language inside the language.** Programmers already write small languages inside strings, and nobody calls that
a style: a regular expression, a SQL text, a format string such as `"%5.2f"` (general knowledge). The field's habit is that each is a
separate notation with its own tool, learned apart. Softanza's rules 10 and 11 make the same move on purpose and everywhere: a range,
a condition, a palette travels as a short string (`L("1.02:1.05")`, `'{ @item > 5 }'`).

**Goals as architecture.** The field's word for goals is quality attributes: the ISO/IEC 25010 quality model (2011) lists eight
characteristics (functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security, maintainability,
portability), and architecture methods rank a system's attribute requirements before drawing it (general knowledge; no file in the
record cites either). The record's seven goals are a different list, chosen for a different thing: not what the product promises its
users but what a feature must serve to be admitted into one library (12.14: a goal is "the unit that makes a feature justifiable or
refusable"). The two lists share the words reliability and, in part, manageability, and little else.

The record's own field study for the style half is 12.16's table of markers per thousand lines (reproduced in §7).
