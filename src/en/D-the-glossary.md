A word in this book names a state of a piece of work, or a role held in a scope. It does not name a rank, and it does not name a person. A crafter is someone who is declaring something, and a person is a crafter on the day they declare. An architect is what one holds in the scope of a solution. A user is whoever is using. When a sentence of this book is about a person, it says what the person did, on what date, and in whose words.

Each entry gives the word as the book uses it, and the chapter where it is shown. Where the record uses a word in two senses, both are given, and they are numbered.

**absent.** One of the three stage words. What the record does not hold, in code or in files, as far as I looked, and I say how far. Chapter 3.

**admission.** The act by which a candidate becomes knowledge: it is judged by the world's rules, and refused with a reason if it fails. Expression is free, and admission is governed. Chapters 17, 18.

**agent.** A program that proposes and cannot commit. In the library an AI agent is a file, judged at load, whose AI model, if it has one, holds no capability to produce effects. It names an actor and never a person. Chapters 17, 20.

**architect.** What one holds in the scope of a solution: the profile, the worlds and the governance. Not a rank. Chapter 23.

**authority.** The level at which an actor may act, compared with the risk of the act. Distinct from permission: permission is held, and authority must cover the risk. Chapter 30.

**bond.** A declared relation between two worlds of a constellation, for one named act. Nothing crosses without one. Chapter 23.

**built.** One of the three stage words. The code exists, a guard exists, and a block ran. Chapter 3.

**candidate.** What a reply, an AI model's answer or an example is, until the court has judged it. Chapter 18.

**checkpoint.** A refusal kept for a person, with its context and a lifetime. Chapter 18.

**commit.** The one act that reaches reality. Only a governed actor commits, under a scope, and the AI model cannot. Chapters 20, 24.

**commons.** The shared ground of a constellation, what every world of it stands on: an identity, a session, a store. It is a world like the others, world zero, and not a special kind of node. Chapters 4, 23.

**constellation.** A graph of worlds, with bonds between them and governance over every crossing. Chapter 23.

**court.** The judge of a thing. A court says kept or refused, and says why in the words of the declaration. A language is born with its court. Chapters 5, 11, 12, 21.

**crafter.** Whoever declares what they know about a world, and owns what they declare. Skill is not part of it. Chapter 2.

**declare.** To say what a thing is before it is built, in a text a court can judge. The act of the kernel. Chapters 3, 12.

**designed.** One of the three stage words. A document says so, and nothing runs. Chapter 3.

**door.** In this book, two senses. (1) The *doors* of a chapter: the programmer, the analyst, the designer, the decision-maker, the educator and the agent, six ways in which a chapter is useful, each a way of using the work and not a job title. (2) The *door* of the foreign runtimes: one governed way to run code in a language the platform does not speak. A door is a runtime and never a syntax. Chapters 2, 16.

**engine.** The Zig library that does the work once, beneath every surface. Chapter 27.

**estate.** My body of projects: some twenty repositories that depend on each other, the library among them, written on one machine under one protocol. Chapter 32.

**facet.** In this book, three senses, which chapters 12, 16 and 26 keep apart. (1) A surface of a declared language: its text, its court, its mouth, its voice, its tongues. (2) A syntax of Haro that a programmer of another language could write; the record calls this a face; this book says facet, and chapter 12 defines it. (3) A tool of an environment's ribbon, ordered by the life of the thing being made. A facet is a syntax and never a runtime.

**fake.** A double that says it is one: a sandbox, a mail sink, a recorded answer. A fake must never ship. A double that says nothing about itself is taken for live, and passes the gate. Chapter 24.

**floor.** One level of the platform's picture, from the engine at the bottom to the organisation at the top, each with its judge beside it. Not a layer of the library (core, base, max) and not a plane. Chapter 4.

**forge.** The machinery that produces a language's court, mouth, printer and grammar from its declaration alone. Built outside the library, designed for it. Chapters 11, 12.

**gap.** What a goal needs and the world does not yet hold. A conversation that asks starts from the gap. Chapter 17.

**gate.** A place where something is refused before it goes on. The registration gate, the production gate, the capability seam. Chapters 20, 24.

**goal.** A wanted state of a graph. The gap is measured, and a goal that cannot be evaluated is refused. Chapters 17, 23.

**governance.** Permission, authority and risk, compared before an act, fail-closed, with the decision kept. Chapters 20, 30.

**guard.** A file of the library's test tree that holds assertions, and that proves a chapter. This book counts a guard's assertions and does not run most of them. Chapters 5, 21.

**Haro.** The language this book writes in: the surface of the former host language, kept and renamed, with a charter and a runtime still in construction. Haro does not run the library yet. Chapters 3, 27.

**house rules.** The fourteen rules the library runs over its own code, five over a graph of the code and nine over its text: a court an AI agent can run before it commits. Not the fourteen rules of writing of chapter 7, which no machine judges. Chapters 5, 32; Appendix B.

**ledger.** The security plane's record of what was refused and why, written by the court and never by the thing it judged. Chapters 20, 21.

**lexicon.** The dictionary of words a sentence may use, each mapped to a verb the library documents. Chapter 13.

**lineage.** The record of a decision with the authority and the risk of its moment. Chapter 30.

**LLM.** Large language model: the AI model the field builds today, trained on text and asked to propose. In this book it is a proposer, never the mind. Chapters 1, 17.

**narration.** The author's article under the library's documentation folder, whose code is the old surface. Not an executable test. (The word has since been given to the executable guards named *narrated*, and chapter 21 says why that confuses.) Chapters 3, 21.

**near-natural chain.** A sentence in which every link is a real call, so that the human is the parser. Chapter 13.

**pack.** A human language as a block of data. Chapter 13.

**perception.** What a person has seen or heard of a thing, named, dated, and quoted. A comparison proves sameness and never rightness. Chapters 21, 33.

**plan.** The one artefact that crosses from a twin toward reality. It narrates, flags and validates, and is committed by a governed actor. Chapters 20, 24.

**plane.** One area of the library, named by what it governs and proved by its own guards: the system plane, the agentic plane, the planes of security and performance. Not a floor, and not a plan, which is the artefact a machine proposes. Chapters 1, 21.

**platform.** The envelope around a solution, and the ground a constellation stands on: capabilities, a commons, a registry, shells, a profile. Chapter 23.

**posture.** What the registry says a service is: sandbox, local, live, conformance. And for foreign code, what its executor says it is: trusted, external or sandboxed. Chapters 16, 24.

**Principal.** The record's name for me, the one who rules: a ruling is written only in my presence, and a session that rules on my behalf marks it so. A role, not a rank. Chapters 32, 33.

**promise.** The line `#-->` under an expression, which says what it prints. A claim about a stop of the program, judged kept, diverged, unreached or unjudged. Chapters 1, 21.

**proposal.** What an AI model, a reaction or an AI agent offers. It commits nothing. Chapters 17, 20, 26.

**record.** The repositories, sessions and files this book ran against, at the commit printed under every proof line. What the book reads its facts from, and never composes. The preface; chapter 32.

**refinement.** The write surface: a change declared with its points and judged by a four-stage gate. Chapter 10.

**register.** In this book, two senses. (1) One of the five forms a reply can take: an option, a data structure, a formula, a sentence, examples. (2) The register of innovations, Appendix A. Chapter 18.

**rehearse.** To run on what touches nothing, and to read what it would have done. Chapter 24.

**reversibility.** The class of an act: reversible, compensable or irreversible. The harder an act is to undo, the more trusted the code performing it must be. Chapter 20.

**scope.** A named frame in which a system act is written: development, deployment, runtime-current. The system acts of a program are always in a scope. Chapters 8, 23.

**session.** One run of an AI model at work on the estate: it proposes, builds, measures and reports in a fixed form of eight keys, and closes with a cost line. I rule; it does not. Chapter 32.

**spine.** The nine chapters that carry the whole argument and can be read alone. The preface.

**stage word.** One of built, designed, absent, printed at the head of each chapter's limit. Chapter 3.

**stamp.** The time a session writes on a message, a memo or a commit. Read from the clock, never composed; an instrument compares every stamp with its commit and publishes the rows. Chapter 32.

**study.** My study of the record: a reading of the repositories, sessions and files, from which this book takes the register of Appendix A and the figures it marks as the study's. A reading, not a census: where the book re-ran a thing, the run is the evidence. The preface; chapter 3.

**twin.** A copy of a part of reality that holds no reference to reality: a twin of files, a twin of the process environment. Chapters 20, 24.

**unperceived.** The state of a thing that reaches a person through a sense, when no person has perceived it and said so. It is a state and not a failure. Chapters 21, 33.

**wise coding.** The platform asks the question the person has to answer, and refuses to guess the answer: Softanza asks and the world judges. The inversion of the loop of prompting an AI model and keeping what runs. Chapter 18.

**workbench.** Where a proposal is rehearsed. It holds a twin, a copy of the system with no reference to the real one; what the proposal would write goes into the twin, and what comes out is a plan. The one a programmer already uses, not a second one for machines. Chapters 1, 20.

**world.** One application, as a declaration with a being, a becoming and a way of being met. Chapter 23.
