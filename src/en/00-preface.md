This is the second book of the AYOUNI imprint. The first, *Refinement-Oriented Programming*, told one discipline through one bank. This one tells the platform beneath it: Softanza, a library, an engine and a language, built to one idea. Declare a thing, and then govern it. A person says what a language is, what a world is, what a solution is, and a court that was born with the declaration judges whoever speaks it, person or model.

I wrote it as a running document. Every block of code in it was run inside the library, at a commit that the page names, and judged against the lines that begin with `#-->`, which say what the block printed. If a block does not print what it promises, the build fails, and so does the page. This is why the book is not finished the day it is written: it is finished each time it runs.

**How to read it.** The book has seven parts and thirty-three chapters. Nine of them, the spine, carry the whole argument and can be read alone: the four of the first part, the three that each state a law (chapters 5, 11 and 17), and the last two. The reader has a button that shows the spine only. Read it first, and come back for the rest when a door of it interests you.

Every chapter has the same nine elements, in the same order. The concept as the field holds it today; the first principle; the form it takes in Softanza, in code; the decision, and what I refused; the court, which is the list of guards that prove it; the field, with what it has that Softanza lacks; the limit; six doors, one for the programmer, the analyst, the designer, the decision-maker, the educator and the agent; and an exercise that you answer before you run. I put the limit in every chapter on purpose. A book that says only what works is an advertisement.

**The three stage words.** Every limit is stated in three words and no others. *Built*: the code exists, a guard exists, and a block ran. *Designed*: a document says so, and nothing runs. *Absent*: I looked, and I say how far. The words are the same on the site, where they have five names, and chapter 3 prints the mapping once.

**What the page says about itself.** Under each title a line says when the blocks ran, at which commit of the library, and how many kept their promise. A second line says what ran them: the runtime of the former host language, version 1.27. Haro, the language this book writes in, does not run the library yet, and each page says so. The engine's binaries are copied from a build made at the same commit, and the page says how recent that build is.

**A draft is a draft.** Each chapter carries a banner that says it has not been read by the author. The marks that begin with `[AUTHOR:` are the questions that the record could not answer and that I could not settle by myself. They are in the pages on purpose: you can see where I stopped.

**The three media.** The site holds the record, with its proof lines. The course, in the library's education program, teaches the same ground in four languages, and by exercises that are judged by running the learner's program. This book holds the argument. Where they differ, the page says so.

**What is owed.** The last chapter is about a line that the record does not yet carry: a solution that ran on Softanza's own engine, and what a named user said of it. Three projects, a restaurant, a bank and an organisation, have taught the library, and none yet runs on it. I wrote the chapter before the line exists, and I will write it again when it does.

The French edition exists as a translation of the English drafts. Each French page says so, and it is read again once I have marked the English page as read.
